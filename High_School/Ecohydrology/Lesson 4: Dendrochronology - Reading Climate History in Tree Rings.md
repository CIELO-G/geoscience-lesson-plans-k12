# OVERVIEW

This lesson introduces dendrochronology, the science of dating and interpreting tree rings, as a way to reconstruct past climate and growth conditions. Building on the transpiration, stomata, dendrometer, and sap flow lessons, students will examine tree ring cross-sections (real samples, cookies/slabs, or printed images) to count tree age, measure ring widths, and relate wide versus narrow rings to wet versus dry years, fire scars, or other environmental disturbances. Students will connect ring-width patterns to water availability, photosynthesis, and long-term climate records relevant to Texas ecosystems.

Relation with Research: Dendrochronology is widely used in ecohydrology and climate science to reconstruct drought history and long-term water availability in a region, often extending records hundreds of years beyond instrumental rainfall data. Tree ring width directly reflects how much a tree was able to grow in a given year, which is strongly influenced by water availability, temperature, and other environmental stressors, connecting tree-level growth patterns to regional water cycle history.

---

# OBJECTIVES

By the end of class, students should be able to:

- Explain how and why trees form annual growth rings.
- Count tree rings to estimate the age of a tree.
- Measure and compare ring widths to identify years of high versus low growth.
- Relate variation in ring width to environmental factors such as water availability, temperature, and disturbance (fire, drought, competition).
- Connect dendrochronology data to long-term climate and water cycle history in a region.

**Essential Question:** How can the pattern of rings inside a tree trunk reveal the history of climate and water availability in an area?

**Key Vocabulary:** Dendrochronology, annual growth ring, earlywood, latewood, cambium, ring width, cross-dating, fire scar, drought year, climate proxy.

# PROCEDURE

## 1) Engage (5-10 minutes)

Show an image of a cut tree trunk or stump with clearly visible rings. Ask questions:

- How old do you think this tree was when it was cut?
- Why do you think some rings look wider and some look narrower?

Brief Think-Pair-Share discussion. State the objective: "Today we will read the rings inside a tree like a history book to find out about past weather and water conditions."

## 2) Explore (25-35 minutes)

Guide students through a hands-on ring analysis:

1. Provide each group with a tree ring cross-section (real or printed image).
2. Starting from the center (pith) and moving outward, have students count each light/dark ring pair as one year of growth, marking every 5th or 10th ring lightly to avoid losing count.
3. Using a ruler, students measure the width of a sample of individual rings (for example, every ring across one consistent radius line from center to edge) and record these widths in a data table in millimeters.
4. Students note any unusually narrow rings (possible drought or stress years), unusually wide rings (possible wet or favorable years), or irregular dark scars (possible fire or injury events).

## 3) Explain (10-15 minutes)

Use direct instruction with diagrams to explain the biology behind tree rings:

- Each year, the cambium layer produces new wood: lighter, larger-celled earlywood in spring when water and growth conditions are favorable, and darker, smaller-celled latewood in summer/fall as growth slows.
- One earlywood-latewood pair generally equals one year of growth, allowing rings to be counted like a calendar.
- Ring width reflects how much the tree was able to grow that year: more available water and favorable temperatures generally support more photosynthesis and wider rings; drought, extreme heat, or competition for resources generally produce narrower rings.
- Dark scars or abrupt changes in ring pattern can indicate disturbances such as fire, insect damage, or injury.
- Because trees respond to the same regional conditions, scientists can compare ring patterns across many trees (crossdating) to build long climate records, including the timing and severity of historical droughts.

## 4) Elaborate (15-25 minutes, can extend into Second Class Period)

- Students graph their measured ring widths by year (oldest to most recent), with year on the x-axis and ring width (mm) on the y-axis.
- Provide a regional precipitation or drought index data set covering the same approximate time period, and have students overlay or compare the two patterns.
- Discussion questions: Do narrow rings line up with known dry years or droughts? Do wide rings line up with wetter years? What other factors besides water might explain mismatches?
- Differentiation for ELLs: provide a labeled diagram of earlywood/latewood and a sentence-starter handout for describing patterns.
- Differentiation for Advanced Learners: have students calculate average ring width over different time periods (such as before and after a known regional drought) and statistically compare them, or research how dendrochronology is used to study fire history or long-term climate change in Texas ecosystems.

## 5) Evaluate (10 minutes)

Exit Ticket:

1. Explain why a tree might form a narrower ring during a drought year.
2. Based on your data, identify one year that appears to have been particularly wet or dry, and explain what evidence supports your answer.

# ASSESSMENT

**Formative Assessment**

- Observation of ring counting and measurement technique during the Explore phase.
- Exit ticket responses connecting ring width to environmental conditions.

**Summative Assessment - Data Analysis Report**

Students submit a short report including:

- Estimated age of the tree sample based on ring count
- Graph of ring width by year
- Comparison with regional precipitation/drought data (if used)
- A written explanation connecting ring-width patterns to water availability and the water cycle
- One limitation or source of error in ring counting or measurement
